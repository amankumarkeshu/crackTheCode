If you've read any system design material, you've seen the **CAP triangle**: pick two of Consistency, Availability, Partition tolerance. Almost every explanation of it is subtly wrong, and the way candidates use it in interviews is *very* wrong.

Let's fix that.

## 1. The precise statement of CAP

CAP, as proven by Gilbert and Lynch in 2002, says:

> In an asynchronous network, a distributed system **cannot simultaneously guarantee** linearizable consistency (C), availability for every non-failing node (A), and tolerance to arbitrary network partitions (P).

That's it. Three terms with very specific meanings:

- **Consistency (C):** linearizability. Every read sees the most recent successful write, system-wide. *Stronger than "eventual consistency".*
- **Availability (A):** every request to a non-failing node eventually returns a non-error response. *Stronger than "the website loads".*
- **Partition tolerance (P):** the system continues to operate even when arbitrary messages between nodes are dropped or delayed.

## 2. The mistake almost everyone makes

People say: *"Cassandra is AP. Postgres is CA."*

This is wrong because **partitions happen whether you like it or not**. You don't get to pick "CA", networks fail. Reality is:

> When a partition occurs, you must choose between **C and A**. Outside of partitions, that choice doesn't apply.

So CAP is really:

- **CP:** during a partition, sacrifice availability to keep consistency (e.g. ZooKeeper, etcd, Spanner reads).
- **AP:** during a partition, keep serving (possibly stale) data to stay available (e.g. DynamoDB default mode, Cassandra default mode).

CP and AP are about **partition behavior**, not steady-state behavior.

## 3. Why CAP isn't enough, enter PACELC

CAP only tells you what happens *during* a partition. But partitions are rare. The interesting question is what your system does the **other 99.9% of the time**.

That's PACELC, formulated by Daniel Abadi:

> **If a Partition: Availability or Consistency. Else: Latency or Consistency.**

| System type | Partition behavior | Steady-state behavior |
|---|---|---|
| **PA/EL** | Choose Availability | Choose Latency |
| **PA/EC** | Choose Availability | Choose Consistency |
| **PC/EL** | Choose Consistency | Choose Latency |
| **PC/EC** | Choose Consistency | Choose Consistency |

This frames the actual trade-offs you make every day:

- **DynamoDB (default):** PA/EL. Fast reads from any replica, may be stale. Stays up under partitions.
- **DynamoDB (consistent reads):** PA/EC. Slower reads, fresher data. Same partition behavior.
- **Spanner:** PC/EC. Strong consistency always, latency cost is real (cross-region 2PC + TrueTime).
- **MongoDB (default):** PA/EC. Acks from primary, async replicate, but reads default to primary.
- **Cassandra (LOCAL_QUORUM):** PA/EL. Stays up, low latency, eventually consistent.

PACELC explains the **decisions you'll defend in an interview** in a way CAP alone never could.

## 4. The interview application

Here's how to *use* this when an interviewer asks "what consistency model would you choose?":

> *"For this system, I'd target PA/EL. The user-facing read path needs to be fast and available, a 200ms latency budget rules out cross-region quorum. I'm willing to accept seconds-to-minutes of staleness on the timeline, because users won't notice. For the **payment** path I'd flip to PC/EC: I'd rather be slow or unavailable than show a wrong balance."*

Two things just happened in that paragraph:

1. You named the trade-off in PACELC terms, instantly puts you above 80% of candidates.
2. You showed you'll **mix consistency models within one system** based on the path. That's a senior+ signal.

## 5. Common misconceptions to avoid

<Callout type="warn" title="Five things to never say">
1. *"Cassandra is AP, so it's eventually consistent."*, Cassandra's tunable consistency lets you read with QUORUM and get *strong* consistency at higher latency. Per-query, not per-system.
2. *"Postgres is CP."*, A single-node Postgres has no P to speak of. A multi-master setup has its own trade-offs. Vanilla Postgres with a hot standby is closer to CP for writes, AP for reads against the replica.
3. *"You can't have C and A together if you're not partitioned."*, You can. CAP only kicks in during a partition.
4. *"NoSQL = AP, SQL = CP."*, Pure mythology. Spanner is SQL and CP. DynamoDB has consistent-read mode. The data model and the consistency model are orthogonal.
5. *"Two-phase commit gives me CAP-Consistency."*, 2PC blocks during failures. It's a CP protocol that buys you atomicity, not magical CP.
</Callout>

## 6. A quick mental model that always works

When you're designing a path, ask in this order:

1. **What's the worst-case staleness this user can tolerate?** (seconds, minutes, never)
2. **What's the latency budget for this request?** (10ms, 100ms, 1s)
3. **What happens during a network partition between regions?** (serve stale, serve from one side, fail closed)

Your answers map directly to a PACELC quadrant. No theorem-quoting required.

## 7. Three examples to anchor it

**Twitter timeline:** PA/EL. A tweet appearing in your feed 3 seconds late is fine. Outage is not. Eventual consistency, replicate everywhere, serve from nearest replica.

**Bank transfer:** PC/EC. A duplicate withdrawal is unacceptable. Linearizable, often via a single-leader region with synchronous replication. Slower, but correct.

**Shopping cart:** PA/EL with conflict resolution. You want the cart to keep working during a partition, but you'll merge conflicts on reconnection (CRDTs or last-write-wins with vector clocks). Amazon's Dynamo paper is the canonical reference.

## 8. The 30-second summary

> CAP is necessary but insufficient. Real systems are described by **PACELC**: what they do during partitions *and* what they trade off in steady state. The senior-engineer move is to apply different PACELC choices to different paths within the same system, strong + slow for money, eventual + fast for feeds.

Memorize PACELC. Drop it casually in your next system design round. Watch the interviewer's posture change.
