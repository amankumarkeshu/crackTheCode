I went through Google's L5 (Senior SWE) loop in March 2026 in the Bengaluru office and came out with an offer. This is the most useful kind of debrief I can publish: enough specifics to be actionable, none so specific that it violates NDA or ruins the question for future candidates.

## TL;DR

- **5 rounds** over 1 day onsite (virtual): 2 coding, 1 system design, 1 behavioral / "Googleyness", 1 deep-tech / past-projects.
- **Total prep time:** ~3 months active, spread thin around a full-time job.
- **What actually mattered:** clean communication, willingness to think out loud, and explicit trade-off reasoning. Not raw LeetCode count.

## Timeline

| Stage | Duration |
|---|---|
| Recruiter screen | 30 min |
| Phone screen (coding) | 45 min |

## Phone screen, coding (45 min)

**Pattern asked:** A graph-traversal problem disguised as a real-world scenario. Think "given a grid of states, find the shortest path under constraint X."

**What I did right:**
- Spent the first 5 minutes restating the problem and clarifying edge cases. Interviewer literally said "great, that's exactly what I was hoping you'd ask."
- Started with a brute-force, stated complexity, *then* moved to BFS.
- Wrote helper functions before the main loop, kept the main function ~15 lines.

**What almost cost me:**
- I forgot to handle the case where the start node satisfied the goal already. Caught it during my own test walkthrough. Lesson: **always run your code on a 1-element / trivial input out loud**.

## Round 1, Coding (45 min)

**Pattern asked:** A two-pointer / sliding-window-adjacent problem with a non-obvious recognition. The phrasing made it look like dynamic programming for the first 90 seconds.

**What worked:**
- I explicitly verbalized the wrong direction: *"This looks like DP at first, but the constraints are O(n) which means..."* This signals interviewer-grade meta-thinking.
- Solved with two pointers in `O(n)` time, `O(1)` space.

**Follow-up I bombed-but-recovered:**
- Interviewer asked: *"What if the input were streaming, infinite?"* I froze for ~20 seconds, then walked through how to maintain the invariant in O(1) memory and what guarantees we'd lose. Got back on track.

**Lesson:** The follow-up matters as much as the original solve. Don't celebrate too early.

## Round 2, System Design (60 min)

This is the round most candidates undertrain for. **At L5, the bar is "can this person own a service end-to-end".**

**Problem domain:** A large-scale write-heavy system you'd find in any consumer product. (Being deliberately vague, exact scenario doesn't matter; the structure of the answer does.)

**Structure I used (and recommend):**

1. **Clarify (5 min):** functional reqs, non-functional reqs, capacity numbers I'd assume.
2. **API contract (5 min):** 3-4 endpoints, request/response shapes, idempotency keys.
3. **Data model (10 min):** primary table(s), partition strategy, indices. *Justified* the choice of datastore against alternatives.
4. **High-level architecture (10 min):** boxes-and-arrows. Identified the hot path.
5. **Deep dives (20 min):** the interviewer drove. We went deep on **caching** and **failure handling**.
6. **Trade-offs & wrap-up (5 min):** explicitly summarized what I'd revisit if I had more time.

**What got the strong-hire signal (per debrief):**
- I gave **real numbers**, not hand-waves: "100M writes/day → ~1,200 RPS average, ~10K peak, so we need ~3 replicas with headroom."
- I named two alternatives I *didn't* pick and *why*. This shows experience.
- I caught my own mistake mid-design ("Actually, that breaks idempotency on retry, let me revise") and recovered cleanly.

**What I'd improve:**
- Spent slightly too long on the data model. At L6+, time discipline is stricter.

## Round 3, Behavioral / "Googleyness" (45 min)

Don't underestimate this. **Hiring committee can veto on this round alone.**

**Themes covered:**
- Time you disagreed with a manager.
- Time a project failed and what you learned.
- How you handle ambiguous scope.
- Why Google specifically (not "why big tech").

**What worked:**
- **STAR format**, but with the tactical twist: I always closed each story with *what I'd do differently and why*. Reflective practitioners get strong-hire here.
- I had **3 deeply-prepped stories** that I could remap to most prompts. Don't try to memorize 15.
- Specific, not generic: "We had 4 services and a 30s p99..." beats "We had performance problems."

**What I almost botched:**
- The "why Google" question. My answer was generic for the first 10 seconds. I caught myself, paused, and gave a specific answer tied to *one engineering culture trait* that mattered to me. Don't wing this one, write it down beforehand.

## Round 4, Deep tech / past projects (45 min)

L5 candidates often get a "tell me about your most technically complex project" round. This is essentially **system design in reverse**, you're explaining a system you actually built.

**Structure:**
- Problem statement (1 min).
- Constraints and what made it hard (3 min).
- Architecture (5 min).
- The 2-3 hardest decisions and trade-offs (15 min, this is where the round lives).
- What you'd do differently now (5 min).
- Q&A.

**What worked:**
- I picked a project I *actually* drove, not just contributed to. The interviewer asked "Why did *you* choose X over Y?" three times. If you weren't the decision-maker, you'll hit a wall here.
- Drew the architecture without prompting. Visual-first communication scales.

**What didn't work:**
- I drifted into too much org context for the first 5 minutes. Engineers want technical substance fast. Cut org explanation in half.

## Round 5, Coding (45 min, "Hard")

A problem that combined **two patterns**, the first half was straightforward, the second half required recognizing a graph shape inside what looked like a string problem.

**What worked:**
- Solved the easy half first, got it tested, *then* tackled the hard half. Don't skip the easy win.
- Verbalized the recognition: *"Wait, this is actually a topological sort because the constraints form a partial order."* That kind of out-loud pattern-matching is exactly what interviewers hope to hear.

**What hurt:**
- Ran out of time before testing the second half manually. Walked through it logically with the interviewer instead. Worked, but I'd have liked to actually run it.

## What I'd do differently

1. **More mock interviews under realistic pressure.** I did 8. I should have done 15. Real loops feel different from solving alone.
2. **Time discipline drills.** Set 25-min timers for coding and 12-min for system design sub-sections. Train the muscle.
3. **More behavioral prep.** I prepared 3 stories well, but had to stretch them. 5 well-prepped stories would have been safer.
4. **Sleep more in the final week.** I tried to cram. Diminishing returns set in around day 3 of the final week.

## Compensation (rough; INR)

L5 in Bengaluru, March 2026, exact ranges vary by negotiation:

- **Base:** ~50–60 LPA
- **RSU:** ~30–40 LPA/yr (4-yr vest)
- **Sign-on:** 15–25L
- **Total Y1:** ~95–125 LPA depending on negotiation outcome

Don't optimize for sign-on at the expense of base + RSU.

## Generalized lessons

These apply to any big-tech loop, not just Google:

1. **Communication beats raw skill at L5.** I'm certain at least one stronger pure coder than me has bombed L5 by going silent during the solve.
2. **Deep dives win design rounds.** Surface-level architecture is a no-hire signal at L5+.
3. **Behavioral is a *graded* round.** Treat it like coding.
4. **Recover visibly.** Catching your own mistake mid-round is a positive signal, not a negative one.
5. **Don't over-prep Easy/Medium LeetCode.** At L5, depth of pattern coverage matters more than count.
