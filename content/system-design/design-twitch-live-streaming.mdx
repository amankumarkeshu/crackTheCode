Live streaming looks like YouTube with a live button. It absolutely is not. **Live ingest under a 1-second-encode budget, sub-second viewer latency, chat fan-out at millions of concurrent viewers, and viewer-to-broadcaster interaction** are problems that don't exist in VOD. If you're interviewing at Twitch, YouTube Live, Kick, Facebook Live, or any platform that streams real-time video, this is your question.

## 1. Clarify before you draw

- **Latency target?** &lt;3 sec (Twitch default, "low latency"), &lt;1 sec (auction-grade), &lt;100 ms (WebRTC interactive)?
- **Concurrent viewers?** ~10K (small streamer), 1M (e-sports event), 50M (Super Bowl on YouTube TV)?
- **Concurrent broadcasters?** ~5M on Twitch peak.
- **Chat?** First-class feature with high fan-out.
- **VOD recording?** Yes, replay after stream ends.
- **DRM?** For premium content (sports rights).

For this post: gaming-focused live platform, 5M concurrent broadcasters, ~50M concurrent viewers globally, 2–5 sec target latency, integrated chat, VOD on stream end.

## 2. Functional & non-functional requirements

**Functional:**
1. Broadcaster sends video → reaches viewers globally.
2. Multiple bitrates served (ABR).
3. Live chat with broadcaster + community moderation.
4. Stream → VOD when broadcaster ends.
5. Discovery (browse live streams, categories).

**Non-functional:**
1. **Low end-to-end latency**, broadcaster's keystrokes visible in viewer chat within seconds.
2. **Huge fan-out**, 1 broadcaster → millions of viewers.
3. **High availability**, outage during a tournament finals = front-page news.
4. **Cost-efficient**, live streaming is bandwidth-heavy.

## 3. Capacity numbers

- 50M concurrent viewers × avg 3 Mbps = **~150 Tbps egress globally** at peak.
- 5M broadcasters × avg 6 Mbps ingest = **~30 Tbps ingress**.
- Chat: ~50M viewers × peak 2 messages/sec on a hot stream = **millions of chat msgs/sec**, fan-out to all viewers.
- VOD storage: 5M concurrent × 4 hr avg session = ~20M new VODs/day; total catalog grows by petabytes/week.

The 150 Tbps number forces every distribution decision, CDN is the design.

## 4. Live vs VOD: the architectural difference

```
VOD (YouTube): upload → transcode (hours) → store → serve forever.
                                                    ↑
                                    "We have all the time in the world."

Live (Twitch): broadcast → transcode (≤1 sec) → distribute (≤2 sec) → ephemeral.
                                                                       ↑
                                            "Every millisecond counts. No retries."
```

**The transcode budget is &lt;1 second** for live, vs minutes/hours for VOD. This rules out:
- Quality-optimal encoding (per-shot tuning).
- Multi-pass encoding.
- Rebroadcasting older segments.

You optimize for latency + cost first, quality second.

## 5. The pipeline

```
Broadcaster's PC ──RTMP/SRT──► Ingest Server (closest geographically)
                                       │
                                       ▼
                                 Live Transcoder
                                       │
                          ┌────────────┴────────────┐
                          ▼                          ▼
                      Origin Servers          Recording Pipeline
                      (HLS/DASH manifest      (writes VOD chunks
                       + live segments)        to object storage)
                          │
                          ▼
                      Tiered CDN
                          │
                          ▼
                  Viewers' players
```

Plus a **chat system** running in parallel (cross-link to [WhatsApp/Chat](/blog/system-design/design-whatsapp-chat), same long-lived-connection pattern).

## 6. Ingest protocols

The broadcaster software (OBS, XSplit) pushes video to your ingest:

| Protocol | Latency | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| **RTMP** | ~3 sec | Universal, every encoder supports it | TCP-based; doesn't tolerate packet loss; deprecated |
| **SRT** (Secure Reliable Transport) | ~1 sec | UDP-based with FEC; great over flaky networks | Newer; not all encoders support |
| **WebRTC** | &lt;500 ms | Browser-native; sub-second | Designed for 1:1, not 1:many; complex transcode |
| **WHIP / WHEP** | &lt;500 ms | WebRTC over HTTP, simpler integration | Still emerging |

**Default:** RTMP for compatibility, SRT for premium broadcasters who care about quality, WebRTC for ultra-low-latency (interview shows, auctions).

## 7. Live transcoding under a 1-second budget

The ingest server receives one bitrate (e.g. 1080p60 6 Mbps). Transcode to multiple ABR variants:

- 1080p60, passthrough (already at this resolution).
- 720p60, 720p30, 480p, 360p, 160p, re-encode each, in real time.

Hardware: GPU encoders (NVENC) or FPGAs. CPU encoding (x264) at 1080p60 is ~5 cores per stream, economically infeasible at 5M concurrent broadcasters.

Each variant is sliced into **2-second segments** (vs 10-second for VOD, shorter = lower latency, more overhead). Manifest + segments sit on origin for ~30 sec, then evicted (live, not stored).

## 8. Distribution: HLS vs LL-HLS vs WebRTC

### Standard HLS (~15–30 sec latency)
Player polls manifest, fetches segments. Latency = N segments × segment duration. With 10 sec segments → 30 sec.

### Low-Latency HLS / LL-DASH (~2 sec)
Innovations:
- **CMAF chunked encoding**, segments are streamed in sub-segments (250-500 ms chunks) before the segment is complete.
- **HTTP/2 push or Server-Sent Events** for manifest updates.
- **Preload hints**, manifest tells player about future segments before they exist.

This is the modern default for sports + e-sports. Twitch uses a variant they call "Low-Latency Mode."

### WebRTC (&lt;500 ms)
Direct UDP streams to viewers via SFU (Selective Forwarding Unit). Used for:
- Auctions (sub-second matters).
- Interactive talk shows.
- Clubhouse-style audio rooms.

**Cost:** WebRTC is ~5–10× more expensive than HLS. SFUs are stateful and CPU-bound. Reserve for use cases that need it.

For Twitch-class gaming (where 2–3 sec is fine), LL-HLS is the sweet spot.

## 9. CDN strategy for live

Live segments are **time-critical**, a viewer 30 sec behind misses the actual moment. The CDN tier must absorb the fan-out without adding much delay.

```
Origin (1 stream) ─► Mid-tier (regional, 100s of streams)
                          ─► Edge (city, hot streams cached)
                                ─► Viewer
```

Two specifics for live:
- **Pull-on-demand from origin**, not pre-push. The first viewer in a region triggers pull; subsequent viewers hit edge cache.
- **Cache TTLs are tiny** (seconds, not days). The whole content set turns over constantly.
- **Origin shielding**, only one mid-tier per region pulls from origin, then fans out to N edges. Otherwise origin can't survive a viral event.

## 10. Chat fan-out (the hidden monster)

A 100K-viewer stream with 5 messages/sec from active chatters = **500K chat events/sec to fan out**.

Design:
- One **chat room per stream**.
- Stream's chat is sharded across N WebSocket servers (consistent hash by `stream_id`).
- A new message goes to the shard, broadcast to all WebSockets connected to it.
- For huge streams: chat room is split across multiple shards; messages cross-replicated via Redis pub-sub.
- **Slow mode** + **moderation** (per-channel filters, ML-based hate-speech detection) reduce volume *and* protect the platform.

For very large streams (1M+ concurrent), chat is **sampled**, a viewer sees only a subset of messages randomly chosen from the firehose, displayed as if they were the full chat. Twitch does this.

## 11. Stream → VOD recording

While the live broadcast happens, a parallel pipeline records:
- **Original ingest** stored to object storage (the master).
- After stream ends, a background job re-transcodes into proper VOD variants (multi-pass, quality-optimized) over hours.
- Replaces the on-the-fly live segments with the higher-quality VOD assets.
- Available as a regular VOD on the channel page.

This lets you optimize for latency live, then optimize for quality post-hoc.

## 12. Discovery: live channel browsing

A "browse" endpoint must surface currently-live channels matching the user's interests:
- Per-category counts (League of Legends: 50K viewers in 1000 channels right now).
- Top channels by viewer count (every minute).
- Personalized recommendations.

Pre-aggregate viewer counts per channel in Redis (incremented when WebSocket connects, decremented on disconnect). Browse pages read from this; cache for 5–15 sec.

## 13. Failure handling

| Failure | Behavior |
|---|---|
| **Broadcaster's connection drops** | Stream is paused; reconnect within X seconds keeps the stream alive |
| **Ingest server crashes** | Broadcaster's software auto-reconnects to next-nearest ingest |
| **Transcoder dies mid-segment** | Drop that segment; viewers notice a brief stall |
| **Origin overload** | Mid-tier cache absorbs; rate-limit if needed |
| **Whole region down** | Active broadcasts go offline; channels marked "offline"; their VOD is preserved |

## 14. The trade-offs interviewers will probe

| Question | What they want to hear |
|---|---|
| "Live vs VOD differences?" | Encode budget, segment size, ephemerality, cost shape |
| "How do you achieve sub-3-sec latency?" | LL-HLS with CMAF chunked encoding |
| "RTMP, SRT, WebRTC, when each?" | Compatibility / quality / interactivity trade-off |
| "How does chat scale to 1M+ viewers?" | Per-stream sharded WebSocket fleet; sampling for hot streams |
| "How do you serve 150 Tbps?" | Tiered CDN; origin shielding; pull-on-demand |
| "How does VOD recording happen alongside live?" | Parallel pipeline; re-encode quality-optimized after end |
| "Why are live encode budgets so much tighter?" | Wall-clock latency requirement of viewers |

## 15. The 30-second summary

> Live streaming is **ingest under a 1-second encode budget → tiered CDN with second-level cache TTLs → LL-HLS or WebRTC viewer playback → parallel chat fan-out**. It's not YouTube with a flag, it's an entirely different latency regime that ripples through every component. The hard parts are the ingest-to-glass latency budget, hot-stream fan-out (especially for chat), and surviving the bandwidth math at viral scale.

Land that with the live-vs-VOD contrast from section 4 and the LL-HLS innovations from section 8, and you've answered the staff-level version.
